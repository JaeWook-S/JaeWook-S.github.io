Project Overview

I am conducting an autonomous driving project using the F1TENTH platform as part of a reinforcement learning course.

The baseline demo is based on a DQN, where the state consists of 1080-dimensional LiDAR distance data covering a 270-degree field of view, along with the vehicle’s dynamic information. The action space is composed of five discrete steering commands: sharp left, left, straight, right, and sharp right.

In this study, I aim to improve the policy performance based on this baseline and explore reinforcement learning methods that enhance both driving speed and stability.

Improvements

Reward Shaping

The original F1TENTH environment provides a sparse reward signal based primarily on episode duration. To accelerate learning and encourage race-oriented behavior, I redesigned the reward function using track geometry, vehicle heading, and LiDAR observations.

Waypoint-Based Progress Reward

A centerline waypoint sequence was extracted from the race track and used to estimate driving progress. At every step, the nearest waypoint and its successor were identified, allowing the agent to receive rewards for moving toward the racing line.

The reward increases when the vehicle reduces its distance to the upcoming waypoint, encouraging consistent forward progress around the circuit.

Direction Alignment Reward

To discourage spinning and reverse driving, I computed the cosine similarity between:

  • The vehicle heading vector
  • The local track direction vector derived from neighboring waypoints

Positive rewards were given when the vehicle aligned with the track direction, while strong penalties were applied when driving in the opposite direction.

Velocity Reward

Driving quickly is essential in autonomous racing. The reward function therefore scales with forward velocity, but only when the vehicle is aligned with the track direction. This prevents the agent from exploiting the reward by accelerating while facing incorrect directions.

Centerline Tracking Penalty

To improve trajectory quality, a penalty proportional to the lateral distance from the centerline was introduced. This encourages the vehicle to remain close to the racing line and reduces unstable drifting behavior.

LiDAR-Based Safety Penalty

The minimum forward LiDAR distance was monitored to detect imminent collisions. When obstacles or walls were detected within a critical range, the agent received a large negative reward. This encouraged earlier avoidance maneuvers and safer corner entry behavior.

Collision Penalty

A substantial terminal penalty was assigned whenever the vehicle collided with track boundaries. This significantly reduced crash frequency during training and improved policy robustness.

Model & Learning Skills

State Representation Enhancement

The baseline model directly used 405-dimensional LiDAR observations. To improve learning efficiency, I applied LiDAR sector-based min-pooling, reducing the observation dimension to 108 representative sectors.

In addition to LiDAR data, I augmented the state with:

  • Current vehicle velocity
  • Previous steering command

This allowed the agent to infer vehicle dynamics and make smoother control decisions. The final state dimension was reduced from 405 to 110 while preserving important environmental information.

Double DQN

The baseline relied on a standard DQN architecture, which is known to suffer from overestimation bias. To address this issue, I implemented Double DQN (DDQN), separating action selection and target evaluation. This improved value estimation stability and reduced policy degradation during training.

Prioritized Experience Replay (PER)

Instead of uniformly sampling experiences from the replay buffer, I introduced Prioritized Experience Replay. Transitions with larger temporal-difference (TD) errors were sampled more frequently, allowing the agent to focus on more informative experiences and accelerate learning.

4-Step Temporal Difference Learning

The baseline used one-step TD updates. I extended this to 4-step TD learning, enabling rewards to propagate more efficiently across time. This significantly improved long-term credit assignment and accelerated convergence.

Steering Smoothing

Discrete steering commands often produce oscillatory behavior at high speeds. To reduce abrupt steering changes, I applied an exponential moving average (EMA) filter to steering actions. This generated smoother trajectories and improved vehicle stability during cornering.

Curriculum Learning for High-Speed Driving

Learning aggressive high-speed driving from scratch is difficult. To address this challenge, I designed a curriculum learning strategy. The agent initially trained at a lower maximum speed and gradually increased its speed limit after repeatedly completing successful laps.

Whenever a new speed stage was reached:

  • The replay buffer was soft-reset to remove outdated low-speed experiences.
  • Exploration rate was temporarily increased.
  • The policy was fine-tuned for the new driving regime.

This progressive training strategy enabled the agent to safely transition from stable driving to aggressive racing behavior.

Results

The baseline DQN agent required approximately 120 seconds to complete a lap on the Oschersleben circuit. After applying the proposed improvements, the trained agent achieved a best lap time of approximately 57 seconds.

This corresponds to:

  • ~52.5% reduction in lap time
  • ~2.1× faster track completion
  • Improved stability during cornering
  • Reduced steering oscillation at high speeds
  • More robust policy convergence during training

These results demonstrate that combining improved state representation, Double DQN, Prioritized Experience Replay, multi-step learning, and curriculum-based speed scheduling can substantially improve autonomous racing performance.
